The models that defined the last few years were trained on a finite resource: the
text humans have written. That well is not bottomless, and the best of it has largely
been used. So the field has turned to an idea that sounds almost paradoxical — having
models generate and grade the data used to train the next models. AI, increasingly,
is training AI.

It is less strange than it sounds. A capable model can produce enormous quantities of
practice problems, candidate solutions, and critiques, and — crucially — in domains
where answers are checkable, it can verify its own work. A model can write a math
problem, solve it many ways, keep the solutions that check out, and learn from them.
That verifiable feedback loop is how systems have kept improving on reasoning and
code even as fresh human data has grown scarce.

## Why it works — where it works

The technique is strongest exactly where correctness is objective: mathematics, code,
formal logic, anything you can test. There, the model is not just imagining more data;
it is generating attempts and filtering for the ones that provably work, which is a
reliable teacher. This is a big part of why reasoning and coding capability has
advanced so fast.

## The risk of the loop

The danger is subtler in domains without a ground truth. A model trained too heavily
on its own output can drift — amplifying its blind spots, narrowing its range, and
mistaking its own tendencies for the world. Researchers call the worst version "model
collapse," where quality quietly degrades as each generation learns from the last
instead of from reality. Keeping real human data and real-world feedback in the mix is
what prevents the loop from becoming an echo chamber.

## The stakes

If self-improvement generalizes beyond the checkable domains, it changes the
trajectory of the field — capability that compounds with less dependence on scraping
ever more human text. If it doesn't, labs still need fresh, high-quality human data,
which is part of why licensing deals and expert-generated datasets have become so
valuable.

Either way, the question of how AI learns is shifting from "what did humans write?"
to "what can a model reliably teach itself?" — and the answer will shape how far, and
how safely, the next models go.
