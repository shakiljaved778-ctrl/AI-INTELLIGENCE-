The fear that autonomous AI agents would eventually be turned into offensive weapons stopped being hypothetical this week. Security researchers at GreyNoise reported that a Russian-speaking threat actor orchestrated **hundreds of AI agents** — built on OpenAI's Codex and a DeepSeek model — to exploit two known CVEs and compromise **at least 440 PaperCut NG/MF instances** at **395 organizations across 48 countries**.

The most alarming figure is the clock. The campaign reportedly reached its **first remote code execution in under four hours**. Education was the hardest-hit sector, with **204 victims**.

## Why this is different

Automated exploitation isn't new; scanners and worms have sprayed known vulnerabilities for decades. What's new is the *orchestration layer*. Instead of a single brittle script, the operator appears to have coordinated a swarm of agents that could adapt, chain steps, and move from scan to exploit to code execution with far less human babysitting — collapsing the time defenders normally have to patch after a bug is disclosed.

That compresses the entire defensive playbook. "Patch within days" is not a strategy against an adversary that industrializes exploitation within hours of pointing agents at a target list.

## The policy backdrop

The report lands as Washington is finally paying attention. Senators **Amy Klobuchar** and **John Thune** are among lawmakers working on legislation to provide oversight of AI, and a former Anthropic researcher's warning has helped turn abstract "AI risk" talk into concrete legislative interest. Offensive misuse of agents — as opposed to far-off existential scenarios — is exactly the kind of near-term harm that tends to move lawmakers.

For enterprises, the takeaway is blunt: the window between disclosure and mass exploitation is shrinking toward zero, and defenders will need their own automation to keep pace. The same agentic capabilities being sold as productivity are, in the wrong hands, a force multiplier for attackers.
