After one of the longest stretches of elevated interest rates in recent memory, the
central bank has begun to ease — and the whole financial system is adjusting to the
turn. Rate cuts are among the bluntest, most far-reaching tools in economics: they
change the price of money, and the price of money touches nearly everything, from a
family's mortgage to a startup's runway to the value of the market.

The pivot is a judgment call about balance. Raise rates to cool inflation and you
risk squeezing growth and jobs; cut too soon and you risk letting inflation
reignite. The move to ease signals the central bank now believes the inflation fight
has progressed far enough to start worrying more about the other side of its
mandate — keeping the economy and employment healthy.

## Who feels it first

Lower rates ripple outward on a lag. Borrowing gets cheaper, so mortgages, car
loans, and business credit ease — a relief for anyone who has waited on the sidelines
of the housing market. Savers, conversely, earn less on cash. Markets tend to cheer,
since cheaper money lifts the value of future earnings and makes risk-taking more
attractive; much of that optimism often shows up in prices before the cuts even
land.

## The uncertainty underneath

None of it is a straight line. The central bank is steering by data that arrives with
delay and revision, and it has to gauge how much easing the economy actually needs
without overshooting. Cut too aggressively and inflation could stir again; move too
cautiously and the slowdown it is trying to cushion could deepen first. The path
matters as much as the direction.

## What to watch

The signal to follow is not any single cut but the trajectory — how fast, how far,
and what the data forces next. For households, the practical takeaway is that the era
of punishing borrowing costs is turning, gradually. For markets, the pivot has been
long awaited; the test now is whether the soft landing everyone hoped for actually
arrives.
