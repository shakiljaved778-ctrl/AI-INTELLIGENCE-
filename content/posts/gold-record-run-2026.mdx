While flashier assets grabbed the headlines, the oldest one in finance quietly had a
banner year. Gold — an inert metal that pays no dividend, earns no interest, and does
nothing but sit in a vault — has climbed to records, and the reasons behind the run
say more about the mood of the world than about the metal.

Gold has always been less an investment than a barometer. It tends to rise when
people are uneasy — about the durability of currencies, the direction of interest
rates, or simply the stability of the system that holds everything else. Its recent
strength suggests a broad appetite for something that sits outside the machinery of
modern finance, an asset with no counterparty and no issuer to disappoint you.

## What's driving it

Two forces stand out. The first is central banks, which have been steady buyers,
adding to reserves as a hedge and, in some cases, a way to lean less on any single
currency. That is patient, price-insensitive demand, and it puts a firm floor under
the market. The second is a wider search for ballast: when investors worry about
inflation eroding cash or about the concentration of risk in a handful of high-flying
assets, gold's very dullness becomes its appeal.

## The case and the caution

The bulls argue the backdrop still favors it: persistent uncertainty, ongoing
official buying, and a role as insurance that never fully goes out of style. The
skeptics counter with gold's eternal drawback — it generates no income, so owning it
is a bet that fear outlasts opportunity, and runs like this have ended before.

For most people, the takeaway is not to chase the rally but to read it. Gold's quiet
record is a signal that beneath the surface optimism of markets, a meaningful share
of the world's capital is quietly reaching for something solid — and that instinct,
old as money itself, is worth noticing.
