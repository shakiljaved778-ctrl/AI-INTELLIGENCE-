One of the quieter but more consequential shifts in finance has little to do with
the stock ticker most people watch. It is the steady migration of lending out of
banks and public bond markets and into private credit — funds that lend directly
to companies, outside the glare of the exchanges. What began as a niche has grown
into a major channel for financing the economy, and in 2026 it is still gaining
ground.

The appeal to borrowers is speed and flexibility. A private lender can structure a
loan, agree terms, and fund a deal faster than a public issuance, and can tailor
the arrangement in ways a standardized bond cannot. For companies that want
financing without the disclosure and timing demands of public markets, that is a
compelling package.

## Why the money is moving

On the lender's side, the draw is yield. Large investors — pension funds,
insurers, endowments — have poured capital into private credit in search of
returns higher than they can reliably get from public bonds, accepting less
liquidity in exchange. That wall of committed capital has to be deployed, and it
has made private lenders formidable competitors to the banks that once dominated
this business.

Banks, for their part, have pulled back from some lending under tighter capital
rules, leaving room that private funds have eagerly filled. The two now
increasingly partner as often as they compete, with banks originating deals that
private credit funds hold.

## The questions it raises

Rapid growth in any corner of finance invites scrutiny, and private credit is no
exception. Because these loans don't trade on public markets, they are marked less
frequently and priced less transparently, which makes it harder for outsiders — and
sometimes regulators — to gauge how much risk has built up and where. Skeptics
worry that stress in the sector could prove harder to see coming than trouble in
the visible, continuously priced public markets.

Defenders counter that the structure is actually sturdier in some respects: the
capital is locked up for years, so lenders aren't forced to dump assets in a
panic the way a bank facing a deposit run might be. Less liquidity, in that view,
is a feature, not just a risk.

## What to watch

The honest answer is that private credit has grown up faster than the tools to
monitor it, and its resilience hasn't yet been tested by a genuinely severe
downturn. For now, it remains one of the most important stories in finance that
doesn't show up on the average investor's screen — a growing share of how the
economy gets funded, happening largely out of public view.
