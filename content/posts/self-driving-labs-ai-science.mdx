The most discussed use of AI in science has been prediction — models that forecast
how a protein folds or which material might have a useful property. A quieter and
arguably deeper shift is now underway: AI systems that don't just predict but *act*,
designing experiments, directing robots to carry them out, reading the results, and
deciding what to try next. The "self-driving lab" closes the loop between hypothesis
and experiment, and it runs around the clock.

The appeal is that it attacks the real bottleneck in discovery. Science advances
through cycles of hypothesis, experiment, and analysis, and the slow, expensive step
is usually the experiment — set up by hand, one at a time, by people who need to
sleep. A self-driving lab compresses that cycle dramatically: the AI proposes a
promising experiment, robotic equipment runs it, sensors capture the outcome, and
the system updates its model and chooses the next test — hundreds of iterations while
a human team would manage a handful.

## Where it's working

The approach is furthest along in fields where experiments can be miniaturized and
automated: chemistry, materials science, and parts of biology, where mixing
compounds, running reactions, and measuring results can be handed to robotics. In
those domains, self-driving labs have already compressed searches that would have
taken years into weeks, zeroing in on promising candidates far faster than manual
work could.

## The limits

It is not a universal accelerator. Many of the most important experiments can't be
reduced to a robotic workflow — they need judgment, messy real-world conditions, or
instruments that don't automate. And a system optimizing within the space it was
given can miss the discovery that comes from a human noticing something strange and
unplanned. Automation speeds the search; it doesn't replace the scientific
intuition that decides what's worth searching for.

## The stakes

If the loop generalizes, it changes the pace of science itself — not a tool that
helps researchers, but a collaborator that runs the method autonomously, turning
discovery from a slow, human-limited process into something that compounds while the
lab is empty. The headline AI applications change how we work. The self-driving lab
hints at something larger: changing how fast we learn.
