In most industries, no single hire changes the trajectory of a company. AI is one
of the exceptions. A relatively small number of researchers have demonstrably moved
the frontier — and the market for them has produced compensation packages that read
like sports contracts, with headline numbers that would have seemed absurd a few
years ago.

The logic driving the bidding is that talent at the very top is not fungible.
Training a frontier model is not a task you can simply throw more people at; a few
individuals with the right intuition and track record can be the difference between
a model that leads and one that lags. When the prize is that large, paying
extraordinary sums for the people most likely to win it is, coldly, rational.

## Why so concentrated

The scarcity is real. The number of people who have actually shipped a
frontier-class model is tiny, and the knowledge involved is as much craft as
science — hard to write down, learned by doing it at the few places that have done
it. That concentration hands enormous leverage to a small group and turns retention
into a strategic priority, not an HR footnote.

It also raises the stakes of every departure. When a key researcher leaves, they
can take not just expertise but momentum, which is why the packages increasingly
come with long lock-ins and why a single move can rattle a competitor.

## The strain it creates

Pay at these levels has knock-on effects. It widens the gap between the handful of
labs that can afford the bidding and everyone else, reinforcing concentration in
the field. It creates internal tension when compensation becomes wildly uneven. And
it fuels a debate about whether the frontier is becoming the preserve of a few
extremely well-capitalized players.

The talent war is, in the end, a symptom of how much value the market believes is
at stake. When a handful of people can tilt a trillion-dollar contest, the price of
those people rises to match — and the numbers, however startling, start to make a
grim kind of sense.
