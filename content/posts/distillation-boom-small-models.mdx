One of the least glamorous techniques in AI has become one of its most important.
Distillation — training a small "student" model to imitate the outputs of a large,
expensive "teacher" — is now the quiet engine behind much of the cheap, capable AI
reaching real products. It is how a model small enough to run on a phone can feel
suspiciously close to one that needed a data center to train.

The logic is simple. Training a frontier model from scratch costs a fortune and a
mountain of data. But once that model exists, its behavior can be used to teach a
far smaller one: the big model generates answers, and the small model learns to
reproduce them. The student ends up punching well above its parameter count.

## Why it matters now

Distillation is what makes the economics of deployment work. Frontier models are
expensive to run at scale; distilled models deliver most of the capability at a
fraction of the cost and latency. For the vast majority of production tasks — the
routing, drafting, and classification that make up real workloads — a distilled
model is not a compromise, it is the sensible default.

It also reshapes the competitive map. A lab that cannot afford to train a frontier
model from zero can still ship an excellent smaller one by distilling from an
available larger model, which is part of why the open-weight ecosystem has
improved so fast.

## The tensions underneath

That same dynamic makes distillation contentious. Learning from a competitor's
model — especially through its public interface — sits in a legal and ethical gray
zone, and model providers increasingly write terms to forbid it. The line between
"trained on public data" and "trained on a rival's outputs" is blurry and
actively disputed.

For now, distillation remains one of the most powerful levers in the field: the
reason capability keeps trickling down from the frontier to the phone in your
pocket, faster and cheaper than a scratch-built model ever could.
