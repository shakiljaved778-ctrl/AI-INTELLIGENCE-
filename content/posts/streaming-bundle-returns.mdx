The great promise of streaming was à la carte freedom: pay for only what you watch,
cancel anytime, escape the bloated cable bundle forever. A decade later, the industry
is quietly rebuilding the very thing everyone fled. Services are packaging themselves
together, cross-selling one another, and offering combined subscriptions that look
less like liberation and more like cable with a better app.

The reason is arithmetic. Running a streaming service is enormously expensive, and
the era of chasing subscribers at any cost has given way to a demand for profit.
Bundling is how you get there: it lowers the cost of acquiring customers, reduces the
churn of people who cancel the moment they finish a show, and raises the total a
household pays each month. What is rational for the industry happens to recreate the
structure consumers once resented.

## Why consumers may not mind

The irony is that bundling can genuinely help viewers, too. Juggling a dozen separate
apps, each with its own bill and its own password, turned out to be its own kind of
misery — "subscription fatigue" is real, and the mental overhead of deciding what to
keep is exhausting. A well-designed bundle that lowers the combined price and puts
everything in one place solves a problem people actually have.

## The open question

What is being tested is whether streaming can reconsolidate without reinventing
cable's worst traits: relentless price increases, packages stuffed with channels
nobody watches, and the sense of being locked in. The technology is better and the
competition is fiercer than in the cable age, which may keep the industry honest —
or may not.

For viewers, the practical advice is unchanged by the nostalgia: watch the total
monthly number, not the per-service one. The bundle is back because it works for the
companies. Whether it works for you depends, as always, on how much of it you
actually watch.
