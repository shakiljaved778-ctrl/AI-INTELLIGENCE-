For years the direction of AI pricing pointed one way: down. OpenAI just bent the curve the other way. The company **raised its frontier rate for the first time in years**, to **$10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens** — up sharply from the **$4 / $20** that GPT-5 charged.

It's a notable break from the industry's reflexive price-cutting, and it says something about where OpenAI thinks the value — and the leverage — now sits. When your most capable model is the one enterprises feel they can't skip, you can charge like it.

## A two-tier market takes shape

The move sharpens a split that's been forming all year. On one side, closed frontier labs are treating top-end capability as a premium good and pricing accordingly. On the other, open-weight labs — DeepSeek most conspicuously — keep pushing size up and price down. Buyers are increasingly running both: a pricey frontier model for the hardest reasoning, and cheaper open models for everything high-volume.

OpenAI is betting that for the work that matters most, customers will pay the toll. The risk is that a higher sticker accelerates exactly the substitution its cheaper rivals are counting on.

## No IPO — for now

On the corporate front, CEO **Sam Altman** confirmed the company will **not go public this year**, even though it has filed confidentially for an IPO. His reasoning was unusually candid: "given everything happening with safety, right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public."

That's a revealing line. It frames the safety debate — and the scrutiny it's drawing from lawmakers and the press — as a live business risk, not just a policy talking point. A price hike signals confidence in demand; holding off on the IPO signals caution about the environment. This week, OpenAI did both.
