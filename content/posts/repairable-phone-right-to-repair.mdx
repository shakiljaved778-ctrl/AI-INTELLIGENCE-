For most of the smartphone era, "thin and sealed" won. Batteries were glued in,
screens were fused to frames, and a cracked display or a dead cell often meant a
costly repair or a new phone. The industry optimized for sleekness and, not
coincidentally, for replacement. In 2026 that logic is finally bending the other
way.

The pressure is coming from two directions at once. A growing set of
right-to-repair laws now require manufacturers to make parts, tools, and manuals
available to owners and independent shops, and in several markets to design
products that can actually be opened without destroying them. At the same time,
buyers who have watched prices climb are less eager to replace a device over a
single failed component.

## Design follows the rules

The visible result is hardware that assumes it will be repaired. More flagship
phones now ship with batteries that can be swapped without specialized equipment,
modular components that come apart in steps rather than in shards, and official
parts sold at prices that don't quietly steer owners toward a new purchase.
Repairability scores — once a hobbyist concern published by teardown sites — are
increasingly something manufacturers cite themselves.

None of this is charity. Regulation set the floor, but companies discovered that
"you can keep this one longer" is a message that resonates with a public tired of
disposable electronics. Durability has become a feature worth marketing.

## The economics of keeping things

The shift reaches past phones. Laptops, appliances, and even some cars are being
pulled into the same expectations: available parts, documented procedures, and
diagnostics that aren't locked behind a manufacturer's own service network. For
independent repair shops — squeezed for years by sealed designs and parts they
couldn't legally obtain — it is a meaningful reopening of the market.

There are real tensions underneath the trend. Manufacturers argue that some
sealing serves water resistance and safety, and that opening up parts supply has
security and quality implications. Repair advocates counter that those concerns
have too often been a convenient cover for planned obsolescence. The rules being
written now are, in effect, negotiating where that line sits.

## What it means for buyers

For consumers, the practical upshot is optionality. A device that can be repaired
is a device that can last longer, hold more resale value, and cost less to own
over its life. After years of hardware designed to be replaced, the most
consequential feature in 2026 may be the one you only notice when something
breaks: whether you can fix it.
