Quantum computing has spent decades as the technology of tomorrow, dazzling in
theory and frustrating in practice. The reason has always been the same: quantum bits,
or qubits, are maddeningly fragile. The faint quantum states that give them their
power are easily disturbed by the slightest heat, vibration, or stray signal, and a
disturbed qubit produces errors that corrupt the computation. For years, the more
qubits a machine had, the more noise it accumulated — scale made the problem worse,
not better. The clearest sign the field is finally turning a corner is steady
progress on fixing that.

The answer is quantum error correction, and the idea is counterintuitive: use many
imperfect physical qubits together to build one reliable "logical" qubit, with the
redundancy arranged so errors can be detected and corrected faster than they
accumulate. For a long time it was mostly theory — the overhead was enormous and the
hardware wasn't good enough to demonstrate it working at scale. Recent progress
showing that adding more qubits can actually *reduce* the logical error rate, rather
than drown it, is the threshold the whole field has been chasing.

## Why it's the milestone that matters

Error correction is the hinge the entire promise swings on. A quantum computer that
can't control its errors can't run the long, complex algorithms that would make it
genuinely useful — the ones that might transform chemistry, materials design, or
cryptography. Showing that errors can be suppressed as a machine scales is the
difference between an impressive physics experiment and a path to a practical
computer. It is less a flashy headline than a foundation.

## The honest timeline

Tempered expectations are warranted. A truly useful, fault-tolerant quantum computer
still requires an enormous number of high-quality qubits and years of engineering,
and the field has a long history of breakthroughs that proved harder to scale than
hoped. Today's machines remain limited and specialized. This is a crucial step, not
a finish line.

But the nature of the step matters. For the first time, the central obstacle looks
less like a wall and more like an engineering problem with a path through it. After
decades of "someday," quantum computing's defining challenge is starting to yield —
and that, more than any single machine, is the news.
