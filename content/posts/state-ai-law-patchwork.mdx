In the absence of a comprehensive federal AI law, the United States is regulating
artificial intelligence the way it has regulated privacy and much else: one state
at a time. The result in 2026 is a widening patchwork of rules — on automated
decisions, transparency, deepfakes, hiring tools, and consumer disclosure — that
vary in scope, definitions, and deadlines from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.

For a company deploying AI across the country, that fragmentation is expensive.
The same product may need different disclosures, different opt-outs, and different
record-keeping depending on where a user happens to sit. Compliance stops being a
one-time legal review and becomes an ongoing operational burden.

## Who the patchwork favors

Fragmented rules tend to advantage the largest players, and AI is no exception. A
company with a standing legal and policy team can absorb the cost of tracking
dozens of regimes and building the controls each requires. A smaller competitor
often cannot, and either limits where it operates or builds to the strictest
common denominator — which raises its costs too.

That dynamic turns compliance capacity into a moat. The ability to say
credibly, to an enterprise customer or a regulator, that a system meets every
applicable standard is itself a feature — and one that favors incumbents with the
resources to guarantee it.

## The push and pull with Washington

The patchwork also intensifies the argument for a single federal framework.
Industry generally prefers one clear national standard to fifty overlapping ones,
even a demanding standard, because predictability is worth more than leniency.
Consumer advocates, meanwhile, worry that a federal law written to preempt state
rules could set the floor too low. The tension between those positions is why a
comprehensive federal statute has been slow to arrive — and why the states have
filled the vacuum in the meantime.

## What companies are doing

Pragmatic teams have stopped waiting for clarity. They are building governance
that assumes fragmentation: inventories of where AI is used and what data feeds
it, documented testing for bias and accuracy, clear disclosure to users, and the
ability to produce records on demand. Much of that work is useful regardless of
which rules ultimately prevail.

The near-term reality is a compliance landscape that is complex, uneven, and still
changing. For companies that treat governance as a core capability rather than a
cost center, that complexity is not only a burden — it is, increasingly, an edge.
