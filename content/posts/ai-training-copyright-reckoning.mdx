The models that dazzled the world were built on a foundation the world is still
arguing about: enormous quantities of text, images, and code scraped from the open
internet, much of it copyrighted, most of it used without explicit permission. The
question of whether that was allowed — and what happens next — has moved from a
fringe legal debate to one of the defining policy fights of the AI era.

The stakes are large in both directions. Creators and publishers argue their work
was taken to build products that now compete with them, and that they deserve
consent, credit, or compensation. Model builders argue that learning from public
material is transformative and that the alternative — licensing everything — is
impractical at the scale models require. Both positions contain real truth, which is
why the fight has been so hard to resolve.

## From lawsuit to license

What is changing in 2026 is that the pure legal question is giving way to a
commercial one. Rather than wait for every case to be decided, many model builders
have started signing licensing deals — paying publishers, archives, and rights
holders for access to high-quality data. A market for training data is forming,
with real prices attached to the corpora that were once simply taken.

That shift favors two groups: the large rights holders with catalogs worth
licensing, and the well-capitalized labs that can afford to pay. It squeezes the
middle — smaller creators whose individual work is not worth a deal, and smaller
labs that cannot match the checkbook.

## What settles, and what doesn't

Licensing will resolve part of the problem and leave part unresolved. Clear
agreements for valuable, identifiable content are tractable. The long tail — the
countless individual works that made up the original training sets — is far harder to
compensate, and no framework yet handles it cleanly.

The likely outcome is not a single dramatic ruling but a gradual settling: clearer
norms, a licensing market for the data worth licensing, and an uneasy compromise
over the rest. The era of quietly scraping everything is ending. What replaces it is
still being negotiated — increasingly in boardrooms as much as courtrooms.
