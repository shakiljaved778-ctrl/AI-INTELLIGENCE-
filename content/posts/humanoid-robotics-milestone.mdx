Humanoid robots are moving from viral demo reels to paid pilots. Several
manufacturers now have units performing repetitive logistics tasks on real
warehouse floors — a modest but meaningful step toward commercial viability.

The enabling ingredient is software. Advances in learned control and vision have
made general-purpose manipulation tractable in ways bespoke industrial arms
never allowed.

## Why now

Two curves are crossing: hardware costs are falling as supply chains mature, and
AI models for perception and control are finally good enough to handle
unstructured environments. A robot that can be taught a new task by demonstration
is far more useful than one that must be reprogrammed.

## The near-term reality

Expect narrow, structured tasks first — moving totes, loading lines, tending
machines — not the do-everything household helper of science fiction. Reliability,
safety certification, and maintenance economics will gate the pace.

## The stakes

If general-purpose robots follow anything like the cost curve of the compute that
powers them, the labor implications are profound. For now, the story is one of
careful pilots and hard-won reliability, not overnight transformation.
