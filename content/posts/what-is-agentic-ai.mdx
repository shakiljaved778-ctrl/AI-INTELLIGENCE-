A chatbot answers a question and stops. An **agent** is given a *goal* and works
toward it — planning steps, using tools, checking its own progress, and looping
until the job is done. That shift, from answering to *acting*, is what "agentic
AI" means.

## The loop that defines an agent

Most agents run a version of the same cycle:

1. **Plan** — break the goal into steps.
2. **Act** — call a tool: search the web, run code, query a database, send an
   email, hit an API.
3. **Observe** — read the result of that action.
4. **Repeat** — decide the next step based on what happened, until the goal is
   met.

The language model is the "brain" making decisions; the tools are its hands.

## Why it's powerful — and risky

Agents can carry out real, multi-step work: triage a support queue, fix a bug
against a test suite, reconcile invoices. But autonomy cuts both ways. An agent
that takes twenty actions can be wrong at any of them, and small errors
compound. Give one the ability to act on the world without guardrails and a
mistake becomes a real-world consequence, not just a bad sentence.

## Doing it well

The teams succeeding with agents scope them narrowly, give them reliable tools,
instrument every step, and keep a human in the loop for high-stakes decisions.
The best evaluation isn't "how smart is the model" but "how often does the agent
complete the task correctly, end to end." Agentic AI is genuinely transformative
in bounded domains — and genuinely oversold as a do-anything autopilot.
