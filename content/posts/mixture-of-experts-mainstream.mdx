One of the most consequential changes in how AI models are built is nearly
invisible to the people using them. The largest models are no longer monolithic
blocks of computation where every parameter fires on every word. Increasingly they
are "mixtures of experts" — vast networks that, for each token, switch on only the
small slice of themselves best suited to the job. The idea is old; in 2026 it has
become the default way to build at the frontier, and it quietly reshaped the
economics of the whole field.

The appeal is a way out of a painful trade-off. More parameters generally mean more
capability, but also more cost to run — and running cost, not training cost, is what
dominates once a model is in production serving millions of requests. Mixture-of-
experts breaks the link: a model can hold an enormous number of parameters, making
it capable, while activating only a fraction of them per token, keeping it cheap to
run. You get much of the benefit of a huge model at a fraction of the compute.

## A router and a committee of specialists

The mechanism is intuitive. Alongside the "experts" — specialized sub-networks — sits
a small router that, for each token, decides which experts to consult. Most of the
model stays dark; only the relevant few light up. Over training, the experts
specialize, and the router learns who to ask. A trillion-parameter model can run
with the cost profile of a far smaller one.

## The catches

It is not free cleverness. Mixture-of-experts models are harder to train stably —
getting the router to distribute work evenly, rather than overloading a few popular
experts, is a real engineering challenge. They are also more complex to serve
efficiently, since which experts are needed varies request to request, complicating
how you pack work onto hardware. The savings are real but earned.

## Why it matters to everyone else

For users and builders, the architecture is why frontier-grade capability keeps
getting cheaper to access even as the models grow. It is a big part of the answer to
"how is this getting both better and more affordable at once?" The headline models
get the attention; the sparse, switch-on-demand design underneath is a quiet reason
the economics keep improving — and why the frontier keeps moving within reach.
