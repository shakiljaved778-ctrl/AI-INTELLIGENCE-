For most of the low-Earth-orbit era, satellite internet had a defining feature: one
company was miles ahead of everyone else. It launched first, launched often, and
built a constellation large enough to blanket the globe while rivals were still
drawing diagrams. In 2026 that near-monopoly is finally facing real competition, as
rival constellations reach orbit in numbers and space-based broadband turns into an
actual market.

The appeal of the technology is unchanged and enormous. A dense mesh of satellites in
low orbit can deliver fast, low-latency internet to places terrestrial networks never
reached economically — rural regions, oceans, disaster zones, aircraft, and ships.
For hundreds of millions of people beyond the edge of cable and fiber, it is not a
luxury but the first genuinely usable connection.

## Why competition matters

A single dominant provider was a fragile arrangement for something becoming critical
infrastructure. Competition changes the calculus: more constellations mean more
capacity, downward pressure on prices, and less dependence on any one company's
decisions for a service that increasingly underpins everything from remote work to
emergency response. Rivalry, here, is a feature, not just a business story.

## The crowding problem

More constellations also mean a more crowded sky, and that is the shadow over the
boom. Low Earth orbit is a shared and finite resource; tens of thousands of
satellites raise real questions about collision risk, the long-term sustainability of
these orbits, and the effect of bright satellite trains on astronomy. The rules for
who can put what where are being written in real time, and they lag the launches.

The trajectory, though, is clear. Space-based internet is moving from a single
pioneer's project to a competitive industry — cheaper, more resilient, and more
widely available. The challenge now is less proving it works than managing the
crowded, contested orbit that its success has created.
