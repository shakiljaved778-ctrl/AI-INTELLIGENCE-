For all their power, large AI models remain, to a striking degree, mysteries to the
people who build them. A model can pass an exam, write working code, and hold a
conversation, and its creators still cannot fully say *how* — which internal
computations produced the answer. Interpretability, the science of reverse-
engineering what happens inside these networks, is the field trying to change that,
and in 2026 it is starting to grow up.

The work is painstaking. A modern model has billions of parameters and internal
representations that do not map neatly onto human concepts. Researchers have been
learning to identify the features a model tracks, the circuits that combine them,
and the internal "directions" that correspond to ideas like a topic, a sentiment, or
a plan. Progress has been incremental, but it has turned a black box into something
partially legible.

## From curiosity to tool

What makes this moment notable is that interpretability is becoming useful rather
than merely fascinating. If you can identify the internal feature that corresponds
to a behavior, you can, in principle, monitor for it, strengthen it, or suppress
it — a handle on the model that prompting alone does not give you. That has obvious
value for safety (spotting when a model is doing something you don't want) and for
debugging (understanding why it fails).

## The honest caveats

No one should oversell it. Today's interpretability explains fragments, not the
whole; the picture is partial, the tools are immature, and a feature that looks
clean in analysis can behave messily in practice. Understanding a model well enough
to fully trust it remains a long way off.

But the direction is meaningful. For years, the capability of AI systems raced far
ahead of our understanding of them, and that gap is itself a risk. Interpretability
is the field betting that the gap can be narrowed — that we can learn to read the
machines we have built well enough to steer them, not just prompt them and hope.
