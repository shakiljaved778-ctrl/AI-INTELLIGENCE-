For most of the chatbot era, AI's mistakes were cheap. A wrong answer in a
conversation was embarrassing, occasionally harmful, but rarely something a court
had to sort out. That changes the moment AI stops talking and starts *acting*. When
an autonomous agent books the wrong flight, moves real money, gives advice someone
relies on, or takes an action that causes genuine harm, an old question becomes
urgent and newly hard: who is responsible?

The legal system is built around human and corporate actors who can be held
accountable. An AI agent fits awkwardly into that frame. If an agent you deployed
causes a loss, is the fault yours for using it, the developer's for building it, the
company's that sold it as a product, or no one's — an unfortunate accident? The
chain of responsibility that's usually clear with a defective product or a negligent
professional gets tangled when an autonomous system made the call.

## Why it's hard

Several features of AI make the usual answers strain. The systems are probabilistic,
so they can fail in ways no one specifically intended or could have predicted. They
are opaque, so explaining *why* an agent did something is often impossible. And they
operate with a degree of autonomy that sits between a simple tool (clearly the
user's responsibility) and an independent agent (which the law doesn't recognize).
Existing doctrines — product liability, negligence, agency law — all offer partial
fits and partial misfits.

## What's taking shape

In practice, the first line of defense is contracts: the terms of service that
disclaim liability and push risk onto the user. That works until the harm is serious
enough, or the user sympathetic enough, that courts and regulators decline to let a
disclaimer settle it. Expect the resolution to come not from one dramatic ruling but
from an accumulation — early cases, new rules for high-stakes domains, and norms
about when deploying an agent means owning its mistakes.

## Why it matters now

Liability is not a dry legal footnote; it shapes what gets built. Clear rules let
companies deploy agents for consequential tasks with confidence; murky ones make
everyone cautious, or push risk onto whoever can least refuse it. As agents move from
answering questions to taking actions with real consequences, the quiet question of
who pays when they go wrong becomes one of the most important unsettled issues in the
field.
