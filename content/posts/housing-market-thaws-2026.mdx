For the past few years, the housing market has been less a market than a standoff.
High mortgage rates did two things at once: they priced out would-be buyers, and
they froze would-be sellers in place — nobody with a cheap loan locked in during the
low-rate years wanted to trade it for a new one at twice the cost. The result was a
strange paralysis: prices stayed stubbornly high even as sales volume withered,
because almost nobody was moving. As rates fall, that ice is finally starting to
crack.

The mechanism is simple arithmetic. A mortgage is the largest recurring expense
most households will ever take on, and the interest rate determines how much house a
given income can afford. When rates drop, the same monthly payment buys more,
pulling buyers off the sidelines. It also eases the "lock-in effect" — the trap that
kept owners from selling — because the gap between their old rate and a new one
narrows enough to make moving thinkable again.

## A thaw, not a boom

The key word is thaw, not surge. Years of pent-up demand and pent-up supply don't
release all at once, and affordability is still stretched after a long run-up in
prices. More buyers returning can even push prices up in the near term, partially
offsetting the relief from cheaper borrowing. The unfreezing is likely to be
gradual and uneven — stronger in some regions than others, and sensitive to how far
and how fast rates actually fall.

## Who benefits, and who waits

First-time buyers, long shut out, get the clearest opening as payments become more
manageable. Owners who felt trapped gain the freedom to move for a job, a family, or
a change. Builders, who pulled back when demand froze, get a reason to start again —
which is ultimately how the supply shortage underneath all of this gets addressed.

The housing freeze was one of the quieter costs of the high-rate era, felt in
careers not changed and moves not made. Its thaw is one of the first tangible
dividends of the turn in monetary policy — slow, partial, and welcome to anyone who
has spent years waiting for the market to move.
