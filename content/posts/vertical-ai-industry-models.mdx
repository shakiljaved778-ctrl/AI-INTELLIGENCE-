For a few years the story of AI was the race for the biggest, most general model —
one system to do everything. The enterprise reality turning out beneath that story
is different and, in its way, more interesting: much of the real value is landing not
in general-purpose giants but in "vertical" AI — smaller, specialized systems built
to understand the language, data, rules, and workflows of a single industry.

The logic is that depth often beats breadth where it counts. A general model knows a
little about everything; a vertical system trained and tuned on legal documents,
medical records, financial filings, or industrial sensor data knows one domain
cold. It speaks the field's jargon, understands its constraints, and plugs into the
specific workflows practitioners actually use. For a radiologist, a lawyer, or a
claims adjuster, a tool that deeply gets their work beats a brilliant generalist
that needs everything explained.

## Why verticals win in the enterprise

Three advantages compound. Accuracy: a focused system makes fewer of the domain
mistakes that erode trust. Compliance: industries like healthcare, finance, and law
have strict rules that a purpose-built tool can be designed around rather than
retrofitted to. And integration: the hard part of enterprise AI is rarely the model
itself but connecting it to the systems, data, and processes of a real organization —
work a vertical product can do once, deeply, for a whole industry.

## The build-vs-wrap question

Most vertical AI isn't trained from scratch; it's built on top of general
foundation models, then specialized through tuning, domain data, retrieval, and
carefully engineered workflows. That raises the strategic question hanging over the
category: is the durable value in the specialization and the customer relationships,
or will the general models simply get good enough to absorb the verticals from
below? Both outcomes have believers, and the answer likely varies by how
specialized and regulated the domain is.

## The shape of the market

What's emerging is a layered market: a few general models at the foundation, and a
growing ecosystem of vertical players turning raw capability into tools that fit the
contours of specific industries. The giants get the headlines. But for a hospital, a
law firm, or a bank, the AI that changes the work is increasingly the one built for
exactly that work.
