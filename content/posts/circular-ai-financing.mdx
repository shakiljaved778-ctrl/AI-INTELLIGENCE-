The AI boom is being financed by an increasingly intertwined web of deals, and it has
started to make even enthusiasts uneasy. Chipmakers take stakes in the labs that buy
their chips. Cloud providers extend funding and credits to the startups that rent their
clouds. Labs commit to enormous compute contracts backed by the same investors on the
other side of the table. Money, in other words, is flowing in loops — and skeptics have
started asking whether some of the demand is real or merely recycled.

The pattern is not inherently sinister. Strategic investment along a supply chain is
old and often rational: a supplier helping fund a promising customer, an infrastructure
provider seeding the ecosystem that will use its infrastructure. In a fast-moving field
where capacity has to be built years ahead of demand, these deals can grease genuinely
useful growth.

## Why it draws scrutiny

The worry is what circular arrangements can obscure. When the same dollars circulate
among a small group of connected players, headline numbers — revenue, bookings,
committed spend — can look stronger than the underlying, independent demand actually is.
Critics see echoes of past bubbles, where vendor financing and intertwined balance
sheets flattered growth until sentiment turned. If a chunk of AI "demand" is really the
industry funding itself, the foundation is thinner than it appears.

## The bull case

Defenders counter that the demand is plainly real: the applications are being used, the
compute is genuinely scarce, and the buildout is a rational response to a technology
that keeps finding new uses. In this view, the intertwined deals are how you fund an
infrastructure boom at the speed it requires, not a house of cards.

## What to watch

The tell will be independent demand — usage and revenue from customers with no financial
tie to their suppliers — and whether the enormous committed spend converts into durable
business. A boom funded partly by its own participants can run a long way on momentum.
The question that will decide it is old and simple: at the end of the loop, is someone
outside it actually paying?
