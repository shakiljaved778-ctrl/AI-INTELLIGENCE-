A new box is appearing near the top of the org chart: the Chief AI Officer. As
companies grapple with a technology that touches every function at once, many have
decided the response deserves a dedicated executive — someone to set strategy, corral
the sprawl of pilots, and answer for whether all the AI spending actually pays off.
Whether the title becomes as permanent as the CIO or fades like some past fads
depends almost entirely on what the job is allowed to do.

The logic for the role is real. AI cuts across engineering, operations, legal,
security, HR, and product simultaneously, and left to itself it tends to fragment
into dozens of disconnected experiments — some duplicative, some risky, few measured.
A senior owner can bring coherence: a strategy, shared infrastructure, guardrails,
and a way to tell which initiatives are worth scaling.

## The trap

The trap is the figurehead version. An AI chief with a grand title, a vague mandate,
and no real authority over budget, data, or the teams that build things becomes a
symbol rather than a force — someone to point to when investors ask about AI, with
little power to change how the company works. That version tends to burn out fast, and
it is common enough that skeptics dismiss the whole title.

## What makes it work

The role earns its keep when it has teeth: control over an AI budget, authority to set
standards other teams must follow, a direct line to the CEO, and accountability for
outcomes rather than announcements. Done that way, it is less about chasing the latest
model and more about the unglamorous work of governance, prioritization, and
change management — getting a large organization to actually adopt a tool well.

## The bigger signal

The spread of the title is itself a marker: companies now see AI not as an IT project
but as a strategic shift that needs ownership at the top. Whether they staff it with
real power or with a nameplate will separate the organizations that capture the value
from the ones that merely talk about it.
