Not long ago, the quest to slow aging belonged to a handful of eccentric billionaires
running elaborate self-experiments. In 2026 it has gone thoroughly mainstream. The
language of "healthspan" — not just how long you live but how many of those years are
healthy — has entered ordinary conversation, and with it a booming market of tests,
trackers, supplements, and protocols promising more good years.

Underneath the hype is real science. Researchers have made genuine progress
understanding the biology of aging — the cellular processes that drive decline — and
the unglamorous fundamentals are better supported than ever: regular exercise
(especially strength and cardiovascular work), decent sleep, a sensible diet, and
staying socially engaged remain the most powerful, best-evidenced levers anyone has.
The reframing from "treat disease after it appears" to "extend the healthy years" is a
genuinely useful shift.

## Where it gets noisy

The trouble is that a real trend attracts a lot of noise. For every well-supported
recommendation there is a fashionable supplement, an expensive gadget, or a dramatic
protocol whose evidence ranges from thin to nonexistent. The longevity market has
outrun the longevity science, and separating what actually works from what merely
sells is genuinely hard — especially when the marketing borrows the vocabulary of the
research.

## The status dimension

Longevity has also become a status pursuit — a signal of discipline and means, with
its own influencers, subscription clinics, and expensive diagnostics. Some of that
democratizes useful information; some of it sells anxiety and turns basic health into a
competitive project.

The sane takeaway is refreshingly boring. The interventions with the strongest
evidence are mostly free and mostly old: move often, sleep well, eat reasonably, stay
connected, and don't smoke. The frontier science is fascinating and worth watching —
but for now, the best longevity strategy is the one your doctor has recommended all
along, done consistently.
