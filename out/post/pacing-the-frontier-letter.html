1,200 AI Workers Ask Washington to Build a ‘Brake Pedal’ for Frontier AI

The ‘Pacing the Frontier’ statement, signed by senior lab leaders, asks the US to develop tools to deliberately slow AI — if it ever becomes necessary.

Europa Research Staff·July 28, 2026·2 min read

More than 1,200 employees at the world's leading AI labs — including senior executives — have signed a statement asking the US government to help build the tools needed to deliberately slow AI development if it ever becomes necessary. Published July 28 under the title "Pacing the Frontier," the letter is notable less for who it targets than for who signed it.

Signatories reportedly include Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and several cofounders, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Meta chief scientist Shengjia Zhao, and Google DeepMind's head of AI safety and alignment, Anca Dragan — an unusual show of cross-lab consensus among people who otherwise compete fiercely.

Not a slowdown — yet

Crucially, the statement does not ask anyone to slow down today. It asks Washington to support an international effort to develop the "technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development" — the capacity to slow things later, should the need arise.

That distinction matters. The signatories are not calling for a moratorium; they are arguing that the ability to coordinate a pause is itself infrastructure that does not yet exist and takes years to build. Waiting until a pause is obviously necessary, the logic goes, would be waiting too long.

Why insiders, and why now

The letter's force comes from its authorship. When the people building a technology ask governments to prepare the means to restrain it, that is a different signal than outside critics raising alarms. It follows OpenAI's disclosure that its tools accessed Hugging Face's internal systems, and echoes earlier calls from industry leaders for an international AI watchdog — a sign that the safety conversation has moved from conference panels into the labs themselves.

Context and consequences

Penalties, enforcement, and the shape of any "pacing" mechanism are all left open — deliberately, since the request is to fund the research, not legislate a specific brake. Whether Washington acts is an open question, and the political appetite to constrain a strategically vital industry is uncertain.

But the timing is pointed: the letter lands amid a blistering release cadence — four frontier models shipped in a single week in early September — that underscores exactly the pace the signatories want the option to manage. A petition signed by the builders, asking government to prepare to restrain them, is a policy signal unlike any the sector has sent before.
