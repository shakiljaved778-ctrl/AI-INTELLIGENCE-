Opinion: Model Fatigue Is a Buyer’s Problem, Not a Buyer’s Excuse

Four frontier models in a week is exhausting. It is not a reason to stop evaluating — it’s a reason to evaluate better.

Salience Editorial Board·September 8, 2026·2 min read

Four frontier models shipped in the first week of September. The natural reaction — fatigue — is understandable, and the word is now everywhere. But fatigue is a symptom, not a strategy. The teams that win the next year will be the ones that build a process to absorb the churn, not the ones who use it as a reason to stop paying attention.

The trap of chasing releases

The failure mode is obvious: re-platform onto every new model, burn weeks re-benchmarking, and never ship. Each release is engineered to look like a must-have. Most are not, for most workloads.

The opposite failure is just as costly: freeze on last year's model, miss a real step-change in capability or a 45% cost cut, and quietly fall behind competitors who didn't.

A discipline, not a reflex

The way through is boring and effective. Maintain a small, standing evaluation suite built from your own tasks and your own data. When a new model lands, run it through the same harness, look at cost and latency alongside quality, and change only when the numbers — yours, not the vendor's — justify it.

That turns a flood of releases from a source of anxiety into a routine input. A new model becomes a pull request against your eval suite, not a fire drill.

Who fatigue actually serves

It is worth noting who benefits from the exhaustion. A market too dazed to compare carefully is a market that buys on brand and hype rather than measured fit. The labs will keep sprinting; that is their business. Your job is not to match their pace but to be ready, on your own terms, when something actually matters.

Model fatigue is real. It is also a buyer's problem to solve — with process — not a license to look away.
