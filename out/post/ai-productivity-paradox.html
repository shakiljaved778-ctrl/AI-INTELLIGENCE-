The Productivity Paradox: Still Waiting for the AI Payoff

Adoption is everywhere and the demos are dazzling — so why hasn't the promised surge in measured productivity clearly shown up yet?

Europa Editorial Board·September 20, 2026·2 min read

Here is the uncomfortable question hanging over the AI boom: if these tools are as transformative as their champions claim, where is the productivity surge? Adoption is broad, the capabilities are real, and individual users report saving time — yet the economy-wide numbers that would confirm a revolution remain stubbornly hard to see. It is worth sitting with that gap rather than explaining it away.

There is precedent for the puzzle. Economist Robert Solow famously quipped in the 1980s that the computer age was everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The gains from general-purpose technologies tend to arrive with a long lag, because the technology is the easy part; the hard part is rewiring the organizations, processes, and habits around it. Electricity took decades to reshape the factory, not because the motors were slow but because the buildings had to be redesigned.

Why the lag is real, not fake

AI may be following the same script. A model that can draft, summarize, or code is useful the moment you install it, but capturing that usefulness at the level of a whole company requires redesigning workflows, retraining people, and rebuilding the processes that assumed a human did each step. That work is slow, unglamorous, and mostly invisible in the demos.

There is also a measurement problem. A lot of early value shows up as quality and convenience rather than raw output — a better draft, a faster answer, a task that no longer requires a specialist — and those gains are precisely the kind national statistics capture poorly.

The honest read

None of this proves the payoff is coming on schedule, and the skeptics deserve a hearing: it is possible the gains are narrower or more concentrated than the hype implies. But the absence of an immediate, economy-wide surge is not evidence that nothing is happening. It is what the adoption of a genuinely general technology has always looked like from the inside — real value accruing unevenly, ahead of the statistics that will eventually record it.

The productivity paradox is not a reason for either triumph or despair. It is a reason for patience, and for paying attention to the unglamorous work of rewiring that turns a good tool into a better economy.
