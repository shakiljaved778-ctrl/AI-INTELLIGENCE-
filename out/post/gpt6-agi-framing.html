Opinion: Be Skeptical of the AGI Framing Around GPT-6

A powerful model is not the same as artificial general intelligence — and conflating the two muddies both the hype and the risk.

Cambrian AI Editorial Board·September 5, 2026·1 min read

When OpenAI's president suggested GPT-6 Astra might one day be seen as the arrival of artificial general intelligence, it made for a striking headline. It is also a claim worth holding at arm's length.

Capability is not generality

GPT-6 Astra may well be a genuine leap on coding, science, and cybersecurity. But being excellent across many benchmarks is not the same as the open-ended, self-directed competence "AGI" is supposed to name. Benchmarks are curated; the world is not. A system can ace graduate exams and still fail at the improvised, underspecified, consequence-laden tasks that fill an ordinary workday.

The word has no agreed meaning

Part of the problem is that "AGI" means whatever the speaker needs it to mean. For a researcher it might be a specific threshold of autonomy; for a marketer it is a mood. When a term is that elastic, invoking it communicates excitement, not information — and excitement is not a spec you can evaluate.

The framing has costs

Loose AGI talk cuts both ways. It inflates expectations that the next quarter's usage data will deflate, and it distorts the risk conversation — pulling attention toward speculative superintelligence and away from the concrete problems, like the summer's security incident, that are already here. Both the hype and the fear get harder to reason about.

A better yardstick

Judge these systems by what they reliably do on your work, not by the label their makers reach for at launch. The interesting question isn't whether GPT-6 is "AGI." It's whether it fails less, on the tasks you actually care about, than what you're running today. That's measurable — and far more useful than a milestone no one can define.
